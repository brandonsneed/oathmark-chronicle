Battle 5

No Coin for the Mountain

Regnal Year 200, Summer

Rabanastre's reach runs far beyond its own walls, by way of keel and sail, up the coast and deep into the western waters, and a scatter of coastal trading towns along that way have long called the king their suzerain. In recent seasons, though, the dwarves of Iceholme have come up out of their deep halls in numbers not seen in living memory, and turned their eyes to the ancestral claims that run from the high mountains down to that same western coast.

Dwarven emissaries went out ahead of any army, demanding the old fealty of every town and holding along that stretch of coast, and for the most part they received it — willingly or otherwise.

One trading town, however, would not bend the knee. Its merchants were foreign to those parts to begin with, owing little love and less loyalty to dwarf or mountain, and no interest whatever in trading Rabanastre's distant hand for Iceholme's nearer one. Knowing themselves too far-flung for Rabanastre's own soldiers to reach in time, they sent word east instead, offering good coin for mercenary swords enough to hold the dwarves off.

The dwarf ambassadors who came calling grew, visit by visit, more heavily armed and shorter of temper, and the merchants answered them with delay after delay, buying time while hired companies were smuggled quietly into the town.

It was Iceholme's own moneylenders who caught the scent of it first, for dwarves hold a good share of the credit that runs along that coast, as they do in most places. When the merchants began calling in old debts and emptying their accounts all at once, a dwarf factor did the arithmetic and did not care for the answer it gave him: too much coin, moving too fast, for anything but hired swords.

Word reached the mountain halls quickly, and Iceholme answered in kind: an artillery crew was sent down to the coast to flatten the town outright, joined on the road by a young witch named Gyda, a völva already spoken of with some awe despite her years, who came not under orders but in pursuit of a vision that had been calling her, for weeks, toward the water.

At the center of the field the two hosts came together in a straight clash of spears, the kind of fight the dwarves favor above all others, and there they got, in the end, much the better of it.

On the flank, the dwarf catapult was drawn up to begin its work upon the town's walls, but had scarcely found its range before the garrison sallied out from behind their own meager fortifications to put a stop to it.

A company of dwarf axemen formed up to defend the war-machine — and Gyda beside it — against a gathering charge of Rabanastre's cavalry, a hopeless-looking stand by any honest reckoning. Their Hersir walked the line all the same, and in a few short words settled both the men and the young völva enough to hold their ground.

The axemen paid for that ground dearly, though they drew blood enough from the riders before the end, and it was not the horsemen alone that broke them, but a company of river-lizardfolk loosed from the town walls — each one built with the bulk and menace of any troll, and fresh from the shallows that bred them.

The fighting there turned desperate fast, and looked, for a time, like it might swallow the dwarf line whole. Still they held — until a blade found Gyda herself, and the artillery crew broke off from their own war-machine entirely, to see her carried from the field alive.

The mercenaries, for their part, found the abandoned catapult a far more interesting prize than any retreating dwarf, and gave up the chase for the looting of it. What might have been a rout became, instead, a narrow win bought at a fair price: the dwarves got away clean, their wounded völva with them, though the field and the war-machine both were left behind.

Aftermath

Rabanastre

Rabanastre has always kept chariots — its home guard has run them along the riverside flats since long before living memory, alongside the lizardfolk levies hauled up from the same waters — but the fight for that distant coastal town made plain how little good a home guard does a holding three weeks' sail away. In its wake, the crown has turned its wainwrights loose on a harder problem than ever they were set before: not simply building chariots for the river roads they know, but fitting them — wheel, axle, and harness alike — to be broken down, shipped, and raised again on foreign ground. It is slow and costly work, but Rabanastre's distant towns, so recently reminded how alone they can be, will have more than hired swords to call on next time.

Iceholme

Gyda's wound kept her from ever finding what her vision had called her toward at that coastal town, and whatever lay in those waters there remains, to this day, undiscovered. It was on the road home, somewhere between the coast and the mountains where Rabanastre's raiders still prowled, that her retreating company crossed paths instead with a wandering alchemist at a small inland town. He offered them vials of some new and untested brew, the kind of gamble only a company already licking its wounds might take a chance on, and Gyda judged it worth the risk. The potions came home to Iceholme in her saddlebags, their true worth still to be proven.

Elsewhere in the kingdom, as Iceholme's claim pushed south from the high peaks, word reached the mountain halls of a lake, isolated and little traveled along one of the critical trails, home to an Eachy and its descendants — a creature of some age and consequence, styling himself Innsjøkongen, the Lake King. Prince Bjorn was sent with his own guard to treat with him, and came home with an alliance in hand: Iceholme would leave the lake and its shores untouched, and send a regular tithe of food besides, in return for the Lake King's aid in the dwarves' continued push south. It has since been quietly remarked, among those who study such visions, that it may have been this lake — and not the sea at the coastal town — that first called to Gyda, who simply went looking in the wrong direction.
