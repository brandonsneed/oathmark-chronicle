The Ravendale Harvest

Regnal Year 200, Autumn

Introduction

The Grainshires have long sat uneasy between the great powers of the Reaches — rich in grain, short on spears, and kept independent for generations not by the sword but by the deal. Their people are shrewd traders by long reputation, and the land itself holds together less as a nation than as a standing negotiation: trade struck for treaty, grain sent west and east and south in good years and withheld in bad ones, each shire playing its larger neighbors against one another just carefully enough to keep foreign armies off plains soil.

The famine that struck Thulemen's Rike some seasons past was felt harder still upon the plains, where the soil had never been so generous to begin with, and the old bonds between the shires — never more than alliances of convenience — began to come apart at the first real strain put upon them.

It was in the midst of this slow unraveling that word stopped coming from Ravendale, a lesser shire among the Grainshires, and what little word did come was contradictory and thin. For a time it was assumed the place had simply burned, or starved, or torn itself apart as neighboring shires had — until travelers passing near its borders began to speak of something stranger: fields and vineyards green and heavy with grain, thriving in soil that, by every account, should have failed like all the rest.

By the last harvest, interest in these reports had grown too loud to ignore, and Ravendale's own merchants answered it — arriving, unannounced, in towns and cities across the plains with wagons of grain to sell, at a season when the earth everywhere else in the central Reaches was giving up almost nothing at all.

The merchants said little of where such abundance came from, and the starving had little leverage to press them for an answer beyond the coin already leaving their hands. By the time winter closed the roads, Ravendale's traders had gone home again, purses heavy, questions unanswered.

Now another harvest draws near, and the plains look no more fruitful than they did the year before. Rumor of Ravendale has had a long season to grow, and grown it has, past anything the shire's own silence can account for. For a people who have held their independence by making themselves useful and unremarkable in equal measure, Ravendale's quiet defiance of the famine is exactly the wrong kind of attention — and the great capitals, who have for years found it easiest to leave the Grainshires to their quarrels and their harvests both, have each begun quietly weighing what interest they might press there before someone else presses it first.
